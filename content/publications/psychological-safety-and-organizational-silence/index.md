abstract: "Organizations depend on employees to surface problems, errors, and concerns, yet a substantial body of research shows that employees frequently withhold such information. This paper reviews peer-reviewed conceptual and empirical work linking psychological safety to employee voice and organizational silence. The literature indicates that silence is shaped less by a lack of concern than by employees' judgments about whether speaking up is safe and whether it will make a difference. Low psychological safety is associated with withholding concerns, with reduced reporting of and learning from error, and, in safety-critical settings, with silence about operational risk. The review also identifies a second pathway, in which voice is expressed but discounted, and suggests that this pathway may teach employees that speaking up is futile. Brief illustrations from the public investigative records of NASA's Columbia accident and the Boeing 737 MAX show how these patterns can appear in high-consequence organizations, without claiming that psychological safety was measured in either case or that silence caused either outcome. The paper concludes that psychological safety and organizational responsiveness to voice should be studied together and outlines directions for future research."
